Labyrinth is a two-player asymmetric hide-and-seek game, set in a dark maze. One player becomes the Hider, who is a living person trying to escape. While the other player becomes the Seeker, a ghostly entity that hunts through sound.

While designing this new arcade game, we were drawn to the idea of a "non-conventional" game controls, yet maintaining the familiar form of a retro arcade cabinet.

We explored different controls, considering all senses that can possibly be used to control the game. Like a hearing-based and motion-based control, steering away from the common key and button control.

Hider

Hider's main objective is to excape the random generated maze using a joystick controller on the left, and a hand gesture control on the right. The right hand controls the spotlight in the dark maze using Leap Motion, which will help Hiders navigate clearly.

Seeker

Seeker plays headlessly. Using their hearing sense as the main input, Seeker will follow the spatial footstep sound of the hider to capture them.

Precedents

While ideating the core game mechanic for Labyrinth, my team and I drew inspiration from three games known for their strong execution within distinct niche concepts. These references helped guide our focus toward developing a mechanic that is both unique and intuitive, allowing players to quickly grasp the system while still offering a new type of engagement.

Key Learnings

1. Designing for asymmetric perception creates tension and collaboration

Giving the Hider visual navigation tools while forcing the Seeker to rely solely on sound introduced a sense of imbalance that supports suspense and requires players to adapt to unfamiliar sensory constraints.

2. Multimodal input deepens embodied gameplay

Combining joystick movement with hand-gesture spotlight control demonstrated how layered physical interactions can increase immersion and make players feel physically connected to the game environment.

3. Rapid prototyping through digital fabrication accelerates iteration

Building the cabinet using digital fabrication tools enabled quick structural adjustments based on testing, allowing the physical design to evolve alongside the gameplay mechanics.

4. User testing revealed how quickly players adapt (or not) to unconventional controls

Observing first-time players showed that unfamiliar interfaces can remain intuitive when visual and audio feedback creates a flow of instructions.

5. Sound design is critical when vision is removed

Developing spatialized footstep audio for the Seeker shows how precise audio cues can replace visual information and become a primary navigation system.
