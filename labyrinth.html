OverviewAn asymmetric game with unconventional controls, in a retro arcade cabinet.

One player escapes the maze. The other hunts them by sound alone.

LABYRINTH is a two-player asymmetric hide-and-seek game, set in a dark maze. One player becomes the Hider, a living person trying to escape, while the other becomes the Seeker, a ghostly entity that hunts through sound.

While designing this new arcade game, we were drawn to the idea of “non-conventional” game controls, while keeping the familiar form of a retro arcade cabinet. We explored controls that use different senses, like hearing-based and motion-based controls, steering away from the usual keys and buttons.

  • Player 1

    Hider

    Escapes the randomly generated maze with a joystick in the left hand. The right hand controls a spotlight in the dark maze through Leap Motion, helping the Hider navigate.

  • Player 2

    Seeker

    Plays “headless”. Using hearing as the main input, the Seeker follows the Hider’s spatial footstep sounds to capture them.

PrecedentsThree games with strong, niche mechanics that shaped ours.

While ideating the core game mechanic, my team and I drew inspiration from three games known for their strong execution of distinct niche concepts. They guided us toward a mechanic that is both unique and intuitive, so players can quickly grasp the system while still finding a new kind of engagement.

ReflectionWhat building it taught me.

Key learnings

  1. Designing for asymmetric perception creates tension and collaboration

    Giving the Hider visual navigation tools while forcing the Seeker to rely solely on sound introduced an imbalance that supports suspense and requires players to adapt to unfamiliar sensory constraints.

  2. Multimodal input deepens embodied gameplay

    Combining joystick movement with hand-gesture spotlight control showed how layered physical interactions can increase immersion and make players feel physically connected to the game.

  3. Rapid prototyping through digital fabrication accelerates iteration

    Building the cabinet with digital fabrication tools enabled quick structural adjustments based on testing, so the physical design could evolve alongside the gameplay.

  4. Players adapt quickly (or not) to unconventional controls

    Watching first-time players showed that unfamiliar interfaces can stay intuitive when visual and audio feedback create a flow of instructions.

  5. Sound design is critical when vision is removed

    Developing spatialized footstep audio for the Seeker showed how precise audio cues can replace visual information and become a primary navigation system.
